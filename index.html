About

Dare Olawunmi is a doctoral student in the Department of Economics at the Peter T. Paul College of Business and Economics, University of New Hampshire. He holds a B.Sc. in Economics (First Class Honors) from Ekiti State University and an M.A. in Analytical Economics from UNH, where he graduated with a 3.81 GPA and was inducted into the Omega Delta Epsilon Honor Society.

His research applies econometric and causal inference methods to questions at the intersection of environmental economics, health economics, and public policy. He is particularly interested in how environmental shocks — wildfire smoke, power outages, climate-related hazards — affect health outcomes across communities, and in the design of policy responses that account for these impacts. His work spans the United States and sub-Saharan Africa, and he is committed to producing research that informs real-world decisions and gives hands-on solutions to economic problems.

Beyond academia, Dare brings an industry and policy orientation to his work. He is experienced in communicating technical findings to non-specialist audiences and in building reproducible analytical workflows that others can extend.

Research

Dissertation · Advisors: Prof. Qingran Li & Prof. Brad Herring

Wildfire Smoke, Power Outages, and Mortality in the United States

Constructs a county-month panel dataset (2018–2023) drawing on EAGLE-I power outage records, Childs et al. wildfire smoke PM₂.₅ estimates, and CDC WONDER all-cause and cause-specific mortality data. Uses a two-way fixed effects specification to estimate the independent and interactive effects of grid disruptions and wildfire smoke exposure on mortality outcomes, with particular attention to distributional impacts across rural and low-income communities. Extends to mobile housing demand in New England, examining spatial variation across co-operative and privately owned land using spatial econometric models.

In Progress
Working Paper · With Prof. Kelly Giraud (UNH, Natural Resources)

Willingness to Pay and Willingness to Contribute Time for Habitat Restoration and Wildlife Landscaping in New Hampshire

Applies contingent valuation methods to study landowner preferences for habitat restoration and wildlife-friendly landscaping in New Hampshire. Probit and linear probability models with average marginal effects identify key determinants of willingness to pay (WTP) and willingness to contribute time (WTT). Finds that landowner responsiveness to restoration programs drops off when time commitments exceed approximately 300 hours annually — a result with direct implications for conservation program design. Manuscript in preparation for submission to MDPI Land.

Under Preparation
Working Paper · With Prof. Ben Xue & Moses Adebayo · Advisors: Prof. Li & Prof. Herring

Wildfire, Deforestation, and Road Traffic Crashes in Nigeria

Examines the relationship between wildfire activity, deforestation, and road traffic crash incidence across Nigerian states using a panel data approach and Poisson Pseudo Maximum Likelihood (PPML) estimation. Explores reduced visibility and road-use behavior as mechanisms. Supplementary analysis uses Google Mobility data to test wildfire-induced changes in human movement patterns.

Working Paper
Working Paper · With Prof. Qingran Li & Dr. Ranjit Bawa

Agroforestry Adoption, Drought, and Net Farm Income in the Northeast United States

Uses three waves of the USDA Census of Agriculture (2012, 2017, 2022) and USDA Drought Monitor indices to examine whether agroforestry adoption buffers the effect of drought on net farm income across 217 counties in nine Northeastern states. Employs a lagged adoption strategy to address endogeneity and uses wild cluster bootstrap standard errors given the small number of state clusters.

Working Paper
With Dr. Funmilayo Akeju

Child Marriage, Schooling, and the Child's Rights Act in Nigeria

Applies a regression discontinuity design to MICS 2021 data to identify the causal effect of Nigeria's Child Rights Act on early marriage and educational attainment among young women, exploiting variation in the Act's adoption timing across states.
